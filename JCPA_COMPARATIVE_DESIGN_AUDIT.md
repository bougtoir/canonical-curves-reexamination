# JCPA comparative design audit

## 1. What are the units being compared?

The unit of comparison is the policy decision — specifically the dependence
structure between the load-bearing empirical proposition in each policy
rationale and the functional feature of the relationship that the decision
required. Not countries, programs, or datasets.

## 2. Why were Fukushima and Kansas selected?

Maximum substantive contrast with a common analytical mechanism: different
sectors (radiation protection vs. fiscal policy), institutions (emergency
disaster governance vs. legislative budget politics), scientific traditions,
temporal dynamics, and error consequences — while both decisions rested on
functional-form assumptions whose decision-relevant features were
incompletely identified. Contrasting instrumental cases per George & Bennett
(2005) / Przeworski & Teune (1970); not representative, not statistically
comparable.

## 3. What is the common mechanism?

Functional-form dependence: each policy's justification/expected benefit/
threshold/intensity materially depended on a relationship shape
insufficiently identified over the policy-relevant range. A named canonical
curve (dose-response; Laffer) supplied decision content the evidence over
the relevant range did not establish.

## 4. What are the common comparison dimensions?

(1) policy-dependent relationship; (2) decision-relevant functional feature;
(3) location/type of functional-form uncertainty; (4) decision sensitivity;
(5) transportability; (6) consequence asymmetry; (7) reversibility;
(8) learning architecture. Same questions asked of both cases; answers coded
qualitatively into Table 5.

## 5. What similarities were identified?

- Both decisions were sensitive to a specific feature of a named curve, not
  to the curve's existence.
- In both, plausible alternative shapes implied materially different
  choices.
- In both, error costs fell on identifiable groups rather than on the model.
- In both, the policy itself altered later observability of the relationship.

## 6. What differences were identified?

- Type of uncertainty: low-dose extrapolation vs. local position/dynamic
  response.
- Consequence asymmetry: severe two-sided health/displacement harms vs.
  primarily fiscal costs.
- Reversibility: limited (displacement irreversibility) vs. comparatively
  high (statutory revision; 2017 partial repeal).
- Learning: thin mid-crisis architecture vs. readily available triggers,
  staging, and sunset clauses.
- Transportability gaps: atomic-bomb/occupational evidence to municipal
  exposure vs. cross-state/theoretical evidence to one state's response.

## 7. What becomes visible only through comparison?

That functional-form dependence alone does not dictate a policy response:
its significance depends on the joint configuration of decision
sensitivity, consequence asymmetry, reversibility, transportability, and
learning capacity. Neither case alone could identify the moderators; the
contrast converts a cautionary observation into an appraisal variable.

## 8. What lessons are transferable?

Identify the exact functional feature the decision requires and test whether
it is identified in the policy-relevant range; appraise decisions under
plausible alternative shapes; match action architecture to consequence
asymmetry and reversibility.

## 9. What lessons are context-dependent?

How much shape uncertainty is tolerable depends on consequence asymmetry,
reversibility, transportability, observational feedback, and institutional
update capacity. The preferred architecture (precautionary staging vs.
triggered adaptive implementation) varies with that configuration.

## 10. What claims cannot be generalized?

No prevalence estimates; no causal claim that all policies using nonlinear
models are similarly exposed; no statistical generalization from N = 2;
no verdict on LNT or the Laffer curve as scientific propositions.
